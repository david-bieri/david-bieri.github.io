\bibitem[21]{Bieri2026a}
\textsc{Bieri, D.S.} (2026): \emph{August L\"{o}sch: Briefe / Letters (with accompanying correspondence from the years 1945--1985)}, accepted (1,100 pages, forthcoming March 2026), to appear in \href{https://www.metropolis-verlag.de/August-Loesch---Briefe--Letters/1628/book.do}{Contributions to the History of the German-speaking Economics}, Vol. 53, Metropolis Verlag, Marburg, Germany.

\noindent \emph{Abstract}: August L\"{o}sch (1906--45) was a student of some of the greatest German-speaking economists of the twentieth century (Walter Eucken, Joseph Schumpeter and Arthur Spiethoff) and is best known for his grand {\oe}uvre, \emph{Die r\"{a}umliche Ordnung der Wirtschaft} (1940/44; transl., Economics of Location, 1954). Today, L\"{o}sch is considered as one of the founding fathers of modern spatial economics, representing the very pinnacle of a century-long German lineage of theorizing about the economic problems of space. Yet, since his premature death in May 1945 at age thirty-nine, L\"{o}sch has remained a \enquote{famous, but ignored economist}, because -- as Paul Krugman argues -- the German tradition seemed to be about geometry, not about economics. This book contrasts this widely-held view with a comprehensive new intellectual biography of L\"{o}sch and a commented selection of his most important academic correspondence. Spanning over four hundred letters from various archival sources, L\"{o}sch’s correspondence not only sheds new light on development of German-speaking business cycle theory during the interwar years, but also on the transatlantic history of European \'{e}migr\'{e} economists after 1933, many of whom he was in close contact with during his two Rockefeller Research Fellowships in the US in 1934--35 and in 1937--38. Because L\"{o}sch molded a rich spectrum of scientific perspectives of this epoch into his own theoretical edifice, his contemporaries consider him one of the most promising minds of the maturing generation of German-speaking economists, attesting him \enquote{an ingenious talent}, \enquote{a very high opinion and an unusual record in non-classical and non-conventional economics}, and the Rockefeller Foundation in 1934 simply considered him \enquote{the ablest of this year's appointees}. However, L\"{o}sch’s achievements are not merely limited to the spatial synthesis of business cycle theories with that of international trade, they also include contributions to the phenomenologically influenced epistemology that reflects the overarching scientific-philosophical climate in the German-speaking tradition of the interwar years. In examining the larger context of discovery for L\"{o}sch’s work, I also show how the \enquote{scientific milieu} of his day influenced his process of theorising, not just the validation of his work within in the contemporary context of justification. Beyond his contributions as an academic, L\"{o}sch’s significance as a historical figure stems from his selfless and steadfast commitment to the principles of liberalism and democracy during one of Europe’s darkest periods in history. Indeed, his deep commitment to humanism and freedom forbade him to emigrate, despite several attractive offers to pursue an academic career in the US. Instead, L\"{o}sch’s intrepidly engaged in active resistance against the Nazi regime, sacrificing both his career, personal safety, and ultimately his life.


\bibitem[22]{Bieri2023}
\textsc{Bieri, D.S.}: \emph{August L\"{o}sch on the Spatial Theory of Business Cycles: Selected Essays, 1930--45}, under contract (450 pages, expected manuscript completion: Winter 2026), to appear in \href{https://www.springer.com/series/16457}{Springer Studies in the History of Economic Thought}, Springer Nature, Berlin and New York.

\noindent \emph{Abstract}: August L\"{o}sch's contributions to the field of business cycle theory and spatial economics began in the early 1930s with a seminal paper on the transfer problem and the Keynes-Ohlin debate. His contributions to population economics, business cycles, the theory of money, and -- perhaps most famously -- to spatial economics are highlighted in this selection of 20 translated essays spanning the years 1930, when L\"{o}sch was a student with Arthur Spiethoff and Joseph Schumpeter at Bonn, to his tragic premature death in 1945 when he was the director of a research division at the Kiel Institut f\"{u}r Weltwirtschaft. The essays in this volume are divided into four major sections, representing the subdisciplines of L\"{o}sch's most significant work. The first section on international trade contains his work on transfer and price movements, comparative costs, comparative advantage, and the location of production and international trade. A section on business cycle theory then deals with the impact of population movements (births, deaths, migration) on the economic cycle and general macroeconomic theory. The topic of the spatial structure of economic development is covered in the third section, with essays on regions, their size and advantages, location theory and price theory. The concluding section contains essays on the theory of money and interest rates. The essays in this volume are translated for the first time, including three unpublished, hitherto unknown manuscripts. The book also contains an extended biography as well as a complete bibliography of L\"{o}sch's work.
